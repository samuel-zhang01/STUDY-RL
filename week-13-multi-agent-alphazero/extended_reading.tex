\section*{Five papers}

\subsection*{1. Silver et al.\ --- \emph{Mastering the game of Go without human knowledge} (Nature 2017)}

AlphaGo Zero. The paper that replaced AlphaGo's expert-game-bootstrapped
training with pure self-play and a single policy-value network. The
algorithmic spine of this week's mini-project.

\textbf{Why it matters.} Before AGZ, ``reinforcement learning'' meant
``train against an existing baseline''. AGZ showed that tabula-rasa
self-play could reach super-human play in three days on TPUs --- no
human games, no hand-crafted features. The training loop is exactly the
self-play + MCTS + policy/value loop you implemented in the project.

\begin{quote}
``Starting from random play, and given no domain knowledge except the
game rules, AlphaGo Zero achieved superhuman performance, winning 100-0
against the previously published, champion-defeating AlphaGo.''
\end{quote}

\emph{Commentary.} The 100-0 is striking but the algorithmic content is
the more important contribution: a unified policy/value network, MCTS
that uses both heads, and a self-improvement training loop with no
external supervision. Every reproduction of AlphaGo Zero (and AlphaZero,
KataGo, MuZero) follows the same template.

\subsection*{2. Silver et al.\ --- \emph{A general reinforcement learning algorithm that masters chess, shogi, and Go through self-play} (Science 2018)}

AlphaZero. The generalisation of AGZ to chess and shogi: same algorithm,
no game-specific code, no opening books, no endgame tablebases. Beat
Stockfish (the world's strongest chess engine) and Elmo (the strongest
shogi engine).

\textbf{Why it matters.} It proved that the AGZ recipe was \emph{general}
--- it works for any two-player zero-sum game with perfect information.
The chess result was particularly striking: chess had been ``solved'' by
classical alpha-beta search for two decades; AlphaZero's neural-MCTS
beat the best alpha-beta engine after 4 hours of training.

\begin{quote}
``The same general-purpose reinforcement learning algorithm achieves
superhuman performance in chess, shogi, and Go.''
\end{quote}

\emph{Commentary.} The key technical change from AGZ to AZ was the
removal of the game-symmetry augmentation (Go has 8-fold symmetry, chess
has none). Otherwise the architecture is identical. The lesson: the
algorithm itself does the heavy lifting; domain knowledge enters via
the environment, not via custom training tricks.

\subsection*{3. Yu et al.\ --- \emph{The Surprising Effectiveness of PPO in Cooperative Multi-Agent Games} (NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2103.01955)}

MAPPO. The paper that established centralised-PPO as the production
default for cooperative MARL, displacing MADDPG / QMIX / COMA on most
benchmarks despite being algorithmically simpler.

\textbf{Why it matters.} MAPPO is what you implemented in the project. It
is the right default. The paper's ablations identified the key tricks:
careful value normalisation, agent-specific value targets, large
minibatches. The lesson: simpler algorithms with careful engineering
often beat more sophisticated algorithms with naive engineering.

\begin{quote}
``With proper implementation tricks --- value normalisation, agent-specific
features in the centralised critic, action masking, and large minibatch
size --- MAPPO matches or exceeds SOTA across SMAC, MA-MuJoCo, and Hanabi.''
\end{quote}

\emph{Commentary.} The implementation-trick list is invaluable; the
paper's appendix has the cleanest description of MAPPO best practice
anywhere. Use it as a checklist when scaling MAPPO to a new env.

\subsection*{4. Rashid et al.\ --- \emph{QMIX: Monotonic Value Function Factorisation for Deep Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning} (ICML 2018)}

QMIX. Value-decomposition for cooperative MARL: factorise the joint
$Q$-function as a monotone aggregator over per-agent $Q$-functions; this
allows decentralised greedy execution that is consistent with joint
argmax.

\textbf{Why it matters.} For value-based MARL methods, QMIX is the
SOTA baseline. Its monotonicity restriction is also the canonical
example of the trade-off between expressiveness and decomposability ---
QMIX is decentralisable but cannot represent XOR-style coordination.

\begin{quote}
``We enforce that $Q_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is monotonic in each agent's
$Q_a$, which ensures that the joint argmax operation on $Q_{\mathrm{tot}}$
can be performed by selecting each agent's individual argmax of $Q_a$.''
\end{quote}

\emph{Commentary.} Variants like QPLEX (Wang et al.\ ICLR 2021) relax
the monotonicity to a richer dueling decomposition; the QPLEX paper
also documents the XOR counter-example clearly. Read QMIX first then
QPLEX to understand the full design space.

\subsection*{5. Lanctot et al.\ --- \emph{A Unified Game-Theoretic Approach to Multiagent Reinforcement Learning} (NeurIPS 2017, arXiv:1711.00832)}

PSRO. The framework that unifies self-play, fictitious play, double-oracle,
and replicator dynamics into a single iterated-best-response algorithm.

\textbf{Why it matters.} PSRO is what you should use for general-sum
MARL (or for cyclic-dynamics zero-sum games like Rock-Paper-Scissors
generalisations). The meta-game is solved by classical game theory; the
inner ``best response'' step is single-agent RL. The decoupling is the
algorithmic insight.

\begin{quote}
``We propose Policy-Space Response Oracles (PSRO), an extension of
double-oracle algorithms to multi-agent RL settings. PSRO unifies
fictitious play (with uniform meta-game solver), self-play (with
deterministic meta-game solver), and double-oracle (with Nash meta-game
solver) into a single algorithmic framework.''
\end{quote}

\emph{Commentary.} The 2019 AlphaStar work uses a PSRO-derived ``league
training'' approach: the meta-game solver is the league-prioritised
sampling distribution. PSRO is the right framework for any
non-transitive-game RL deployment.

\section*{Bonus paper: Vinyals et al.\ --- \emph{Grandmaster level in StarCraft II using multi-agent reinforcement learning} (Nature 2019)}

AlphaStar. The most ambitious public MARL system to date. League training
with main agents, main exploiters, league exploiters; supervised
pre-training from human games; large-scale distributed self-play.

\textbf{Why it matters.} Pulls together everything in this week: CTDE
(actors take local observations, critics take the global state), self-play
(at the league level), and best-response training (per main agent).
The Nature paper has the cleanest published account of league training.

\begin{quote}
``Without the additional exploiters in the league, the main agents
collapse onto rock-paper-scissors strategies. The diversity of the
league is the key to robust play.''
\end{quote}

\emph{Commentary.} The collapse-onto-RPS-cycles finding is the
canonical example of why naive self-play fails in non-transitive games.
The fix --- explicit exploiters --- is structural, not algorithmic.
Any production multi-agent RL system on real-world economic games will
need a similar structural fix.

\section*{Bonus paper: Schrittwieser et al.\ --- \emph{Mastering Atari, Go, chess and shogi by planning with a learned model} (Nature 2020)}

MuZero. Extends AlphaZero to environments where the dynamics are
unknown: the agent learns a latent dynamics model alongside the policy
and value, and does MCTS in the latent space.

\textbf{Why it matters.} It is the single algorithm that handles both
board games (perfect-info, known transitions) and Atari (pixel inputs,
unknown transitions). MuZero set a new SOTA on Atari while matching
AlphaZero on the board games.

\begin{quote}
``MuZero matches the performance of AlphaZero in Go, chess and shogi,
and improves upon the state of the art in Atari without any
prior knowledge of the game rules.''
\end{quote}

\emph{Commentary.} The latent-dynamics model is trained end-to-end
with policy and value losses. EfficientZero (Ye et al.\ 2021) shows
that a self-supervised consistency loss between predicted and actual
latents accelerates this further; the data efficiency improvements
are dramatic ($\sim 100\times$ less data for comparable Atari scores).

\section*{Glossary}

\begin{description}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item[CTDE.] Centralised Training, Decentralised Execution. Critic
  sees all; actor sees local observation only.
  \item[PUCT.] Predictor + UCB applied to Trees; the AlphaZero
  selection rule.
  \item[Dirichlet noise.] Exploration noise added to the prior at the
  MCTS root only. Essential to prevent policy collapse.
  \item[Symmetry augmentation.] For games with board symmetries, each
  training tuple is augmented with its 8 (or 4, or 2) symmetric
  versions.
  \item[Self-play.] Both agents in a 2-player game share the same
  policy network; the network plays itself.
  \item[Fictitious play.] Each agent best-responds to the empirical
  distribution of historical opponent actions.
  \item[PSRO.] Policy-Space Response Oracles; meta-game over a policy
  population.
  \item[League training.] AlphaStar's PSRO variant with main agents
  and explicit exploiter populations.
  \item[QMIX monotonicity.] The aggregator from per-agent $Q$s to joint
  $Q$ is monotone, which makes decentralised greedy consistent with
  joint argmax.
  \item[MuZero.] AlphaZero with a learned latent dynamics model.
\end{description}

\section*{Historical context: how we got here}

The thread from Samuel's checkers program (1959) to AlphaZero is a
60-year story of intelligent search getting steadily better priors.
The milestones:

\textbf{1959 --- Samuel's checkers.} Self-play with hand-crafted
features; the first ML system to demonstrate genuine improvement
through play. Samuel's machine learned its evaluation function via TD-like
updates on tens of thousands of self-play games.

\textbf{1992 --- TD-Gammon (Tesauro).} TD($\lambda$) on a neural-net
backgammon evaluator. World-class play on a single workstation; first
demonstration that neural nets + TD + self-play could match expert
human play in a complex domain.

\textbf{1997 --- Deep Blue beats Kasparov.} Hand-crafted features +
massive alpha-beta search. The end of the ``classical'' chess era.

\textbf{2007 --- MCTS (UCT).} Kocsis \& Szepesv\'ari; the algorithm
that gave bandit-style exploration to game tree search. Powered the
first super-human computer Go programs (CrazyStone, Zen).

\textbf{2013 --- DQN (Mnih et al.).} Deep RL on Atari. Proved that
neural nets + replay buffer + target network could learn pixel-to-action
mappings.

\textbf{2016 --- AlphaGo.} MCTS + policy network trained on human games
+ value network. Beat Lee Sedol 4-1.

\textbf{2017 --- AlphaGo Zero.} Removed the human-game bootstrap; pure
self-play. The algorithm of this week's mini-project.

\textbf{2018 --- AlphaZero.} Generalised AGZ to chess, shogi, and Go
with a single algorithm. The proof that the recipe is domain-agnostic.

\textbf{2019 --- AlphaStar.} League training for non-transitive games.
StarCraft II at grandmaster level.

\textbf{2020 --- MuZero.} Latent dynamics model; AlphaZero without
knowing the rules. Atari-level play with the same algorithm that masters
Go.

\textbf{2022 --- MAPPO surprise.} The simplest CTDE PPO matches or
beats every more-sophisticated MARL algorithm on the standard
benchmarks. Lesson: implementation engineering matters more than
algorithmic sophistication.

\section*{Annotated reading order}

\textbf{Weekend 1 (foundations).} Silver et al.\ Nature 2017 (AlphaGo
Zero) end-to-end, then Silver et al.\ Science 2018 (AlphaZero). Both
papers are short; the supplementary materials are where the
implementation details live --- read those.

\textbf{Weekend 2 (cooperative MARL).} Yu et al.\ NeurIPS 2022 (MAPPO);
Rashid et al.\ ICML 2018 (QMIX); Lowe et al.\ NeurIPS 2017 (MADDPG).
Together these give you the three flavours of CTDE: PPO-style on-policy,
DQN-style value-decomposition, DDPG-style off-policy.

\textbf{Weekend 3 (competitive / general-sum).} Lanctot et al.\ NeurIPS
2017 (PSRO); Vinyals et al.\ Nature 2019 (AlphaStar); Brown \& Sandholm
Science 2017 (Libratus, poker CFR). The full picture of how to handle
non-transitive games at scale.

\textbf{Weekend 4 (model-based extensions).} Schrittwieser et al.\
Nature 2020 (MuZero); Ye et al.\ NeurIPS 2021 (EfficientZero); Antonoglou
et al.\ ICLR 2022 (Stochastic MuZero).

\section*{Codebase tour}

If you want to read more code than you write, three open-source
AlphaZero / MuZero implementations are worth knowing:

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \textbf{KataGo} (\url{https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo}):
    The strongest open-source Go engine; descended from AlphaGo Zero.
    The training-data pipeline is particularly well-engineered.
  \item \textbf{OpenSpiel} (\url{https://github.com/deepmind/open\_spiel}):
    DeepMind's game-RL library; has clean implementations of CFR, PSRO,
    AlphaZero, and dozens of games.
  \item \textbf{MuZero-General} (\url{https://github.com/werner-duvaud/muzero-general}):
    A clean MuZero reproduction with a tiny code footprint; recommended
    starting point for understanding the MuZero codebase.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Open problems}

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item \emph{Continuous-action AlphaZero}: MCTS with infinite action
    spaces. Progressive widening (Couetoux et al.\ 2011), neural MCTS
    over latent action distributions; still an open area.
  \item \emph{Partial-information AlphaZero}: poker-style hidden
    information; CFR + neural nets is the state of the art (DeepStack,
    Libratus, Pluribus), but unifying with the AlphaZero recipe is
    still an active research direction.
  \item \emph{Cooperative MARL with strategic agents}: most MARL
    benchmarks assume cooperative or zero-sum. Mixed-incentive cases
    (synthetic insurance brokers vs.\ platform; ATS recruiters vs.\ candidates) need
    mechanism-design awareness layered on top.
\end{itemize}
